ALGEBRA 2 · A VISUAL GUIDE
The Graph Shapes
Every function in Algebra 2 has a personality. Once you know the personality, you can read the equation without graphing it.

Use the sliders to change the parameters. Hover the graph to read x and y values.
And here's the secret running through all of it:
every question in algebra is asking one thing —
given the output, what was the input?
01 The Steady Walker The Linear Function

Never changes pace. Same slope everywhere. No peaks, no valleys, no drama. Just a constant rate, forever. It's the simplest shape in the book, but most of the real world runs on it.

If you know the slope and one point, you know everything. That's a very short signature.

domain: all realsrange: all realsno asymptotesexactly 1 zero
The question: given y, find x. That's x = (y − b) / m. Always exactly one answer. The only shape where the question is this clean.

02 The Bouncer The Parabola

Thrown upward, hits a peak, falls back down. Perfectly symmetric — mirror the left side and it exactly matches the right. The most common shape in physics.

The vertex is everything. The discriminant (b²−4ac) is the mood ring: positive = two crossings, zero = one, negative = never touches zero.

domain: all realsrange: [k, ∞) or (−∞, k]vertex (h, k)0, 1, or 2 zeros
The question: where does it hit zero? That's what the quadratic formula answers — and completing the square, and factoring. Three methods, same question.

03 The V-Shape The Absolute Value Function

Always positive. Folds the number line in half at its vertex. Whatever was negative gets reflected. Walk left and the output rises just as fast as walking right.

domain: all realsrange: [k, ∞)vertex (h, k)two branches
The question: what two inputs give this output? The V has two branches — isolate first, then split into positive and negative cases. Always check both.
Absolute Value Grapher Equation Stepper
04 The Half-Curve The Radical Function

Only starts once the inside isn't negative. Before the starting point, it simply doesn't exist. After it grows — but slowly, slower and slower forever.

The starting point (h, k) is everything. The domain begins there. Everything to the left is forbidden.

domain: x ≥ hrange: y ≥ kstarts at (h, k)
The question: what's the inverse of squaring? Almost √x — except squaring destroys information, so the inverse can only recover one branch. That's where extraneous solutions come from.

05 The Hockey Stick The Exponential Function

Slow at first, then suddenly unstoppable. Growth compounds on itself. This is the shape of every population, every interest account, every virus. The horizontal asymptote is the floor it never falls through.

domain: all realsrange: above asymptotenever crosses asymptote
The question: given the output, what was the exponent? That question is so important it gets its own function: the logarithm.
Exponential Explorer Model Builder Growth Race
06 The Leveler The Logarithm

Starts by growing fast, then gradually slows to almost nothing — the exact opposite personality of its twin the exponential. Vertical asymptote on the left: the log of zero is undefined.

domain: x > hrange: all realsvertical asymptote at x = h
The question: the logarithm is the answer to the exponential's question. log_b(y) = x means "b to what power gives y?" Every log problem is that in disguise.
Log ↔ Exp Converter Mirror Explorer Log Properties
07 The One With Walls The Rational Function

Has forbidden zones — inputs that make the denominator zero. Approaches invisible walls it can never cross. Races toward infinity near a vertical asymptote. Levels off near the horizontal one, approaching but never arriving.

V.A. where denom = 0H.A. from degreesholes where factors cancel
The question: where are the walls, and why? Solving rational equations requires clearing denominators — and that can create extraneous solutions near the walls. Always check.
Rational Function Behavior Solving Rational Equations
08 The Rollercoaster The Polynomial

Can go anywhere, turn as many times as it wants — up to degree minus one turns. No asymptotes. No forbidden zones. Crosses zero at its roots, or bounces if the root has even multiplicity. Complex roots always come in pairs.

domain: all realsmax turns: degree − 1cross or bounce at roots
The question: where does it cross zero? Factoring, long division, synthetic division — all different tools hunting for the same thing.
Polynomial Sketch Pad Polynomial Root Sculptor Division Stepper
09 The Repeater The Trig Function

Goes forever. Never stops. The exact same cycle, repeated endlessly. Bounded: no matter what you put in, the output stays between its max and min. The only shape that is both infinite and constrained.

domain: all realsrange: [k−|a|, k+|a|]period: 2π / b
The question: where are you in the cycle? The unit circle is the answer. Sine and cosine are just the y and x coordinates of a point traveling around a circle. The wave is the circle, unrolled.

10 The Cross-Sections The Conic Sections

Four completely different-looking curves that all come from slicing the same double cone at different angles. Circle. Ellipse. Parabola. Hyperbola. One source, four shapes.

They all share the same language: (h, k) is the center or vertex. Plus between squared terms means ellipse. Minus means hyperbola. One variable squared means parabola.

center/vertex (h, k)+ → ellipse/circle− → hyperbolaone squared → parabola
The question: what cross-section did you take? Read the equation, count squared terms, check the sign, identify h and k. The parabola was a conic all along.
Conic Sections Explorer Quadratic Forms Explorer
They're all related.

The logarithm and the exponential are exact mirrors of each other — reflect one over y = x and you get the other.

The parabola is a conic section. It was always a slice of a cone, even in September when you first met it as y = ax² + bx + c.

The asymptote of the rational function and the domain restriction of the radical are the same idea: some inputs are forbidden. The same wall, different rooms.

By the end of this year, you can look at any equation and ask:
What kind of shape is this? What's its personality?

And whatever question the problem is asking — it's always the same move:

given the output, what was the input?

That's algebra.
