ALGEBRA 2 · A VISUAL GUIDE
The Graph Shapes
Every function in Algebra 2 has a personality. Once you know the personality, you can read the equation without graphing it — the way you recognize a friend from across the room before you can see their face.

Hover over any graph to read x and y values.
And here's the secret running through all of it:
every question in algebra is asking one thing —
given the output, what was the input?
01 The Steady Walker The Linear Function  ·  y = mx + b
hover to read values

Never changes pace. Same slope everywhere. No peaks, no valleys, no drama. Just a constant rate, forever — uphill or downhill or perfectly flat. It's the simplest shape in the book, but don't underestimate it. Most of the real world runs on it.

If you know the slope and one point, you know everything. That's a very short signature.

domain: all reals range: all reals no asymptotes exactly 1 zero
The question: given y, find x. That's x = (y − b) / m. Always exactly one answer. The linear function is the only shape where the question is this clean.

02 The Bouncer The Parabola  ·  y = a(x−h)² + k
hover to read values

Thrown upward, hits a peak, falls back down. Or flipped: dips to a bottom, rises again. Perfectly symmetric — mirror the left side and it exactly matches the right. The most common shape in physics.

The vertex is everything. The a value tells you which way and how narrow. The discriminant (b²−4ac) is the bouncer's mood ring: positive means two crossings, zero means one, negative means it never touches zero.

domain: all reals range: [k, ∞) or (−∞, k] vertex (h, k) 0, 1, or 2 zeros
The question: where does it hit zero? That's what the quadratic formula answers — and completing the square, and factoring. Three methods, same question. When the answer is imaginary, the parabola never touches the x-axis.

03 The V-Shape The Absolute Value Function  ·  y = a|x−h| + k
hover to read values

Always positive. Folds the number line in half at its vertex. Whatever was negative gets reflected — the distance from zero, not the signed value. Walk left and the output rises just as fast as walking right.

domain: all reals range: [k, ∞) vertex (h, k) two branches
The question: what two inputs give this output? The V has two branches — so there are always two candidates. That's the split: isolate first, then split into positive and negative cases. Always check both.

04 The Half-Curve The Radical Function  ·  y = a√(x−h) + k
hover to read values

Only starts once the inside isn't negative. Before the starting point, it simply doesn't exist — not zero, not defined. After the start it grows, but slowly, slower and slower forever.

The starting point (h, k) is everything. The domain begins there. Everything to the left is forbidden.

domain: x ≥ h range: y ≥ k starts at (h, k) always increasing
The question: what's the inverse of squaring? The answer is almost √x — except squaring destroys information (both 3 and −3 square to 9), so the inverse can only recover one branch. That's why extraneous solutions appear when you square both sides.

05 The Hockey Stick The Exponential Function  ·  y = a · bˣ
hover to read values

Slow at first, then suddenly unstoppable. Growth compounds on itself — each step is proportional to where you already are, not fixed like the linear function. This is the shape of every population, every interest account, every virus.

The horizontal asymptote is the floor it never falls through. Base b greater than 1 means growth. Between 0 and 1 means decay. Base 1 is a flat line — not exponential at all.

domain: all reals range: above asymptote y-intercept: a never crosses asymptote
The question: given the output, what was the exponent? That question is so important it gets its own function: the logarithm. The exponential and the logarithm are twins — inverses of each other. Given y = bˣ, solve for x.

06 The Leveler The Logarithm  ·  y = log_b(x)
hover to read values

Starts by growing fast, then gradually slows to almost nothing — the exact opposite personality of its twin. It takes enormous inputs to push the output up even a little. The Richter scale, decibels, pH — all logarithmic.

Vertical asymptote on the left: the log of zero is undefined. Domain starts just to the right of that wall.

domain: x > 0 range: all reals vertical asymptote at x = 0 x-intercept at x = 1
The question: the logarithm is the answer to the exponential's question. log_b(y) = x means "b to what power gives y?" Every log problem is that question in disguise.

07 The One With Walls The Rational Function  ·  y = p(x) / q(x)
hover to read values

Has forbidden zones — inputs that make the denominator zero. Approaches invisible walls it can never cross. Near a vertical asymptote, the function races toward infinity. Near a horizontal asymptote, it slows and levels off, approaching but never arriving.

Some walls are real (vertical asymptotes). Some are illusions that cancel out (holes). The distinction matters.

V.A. where denom = 0 H.A. from degrees holes where factors cancel excluded values
The question: where are the walls, and why? Given an output, what input gets there? Solving rational equations requires clearing denominators — and that can create extraneous solutions near the walls. Always check.

08 The Rollercoaster The Polynomial  ·  y = aₙxⁿ + … + a₀
hover to read values

Can go anywhere, turn as many times as it wants — up to degree minus one turns. No asymptotes. No forbidden zones. Just smooth curves crossing and bouncing off the x-axis wherever its roots land.

Odd multiplicity root: the curve crosses through. Even multiplicity: it bounces. Complex roots always come in pairs and leave no trace on the real graph.

domain: all reals max turns: degree − 1 cross or bounce at roots complex roots in pairs
The question: where does it cross zero? Factoring, long division, synthetic division — all different tools hunting for the same thing. The parabola (degree 2) is the simplest polynomial, and the quadratic formula is the polynomial's most famous zero-hunter.

09 The Repeater The Trig Function  ·  y = a · sin(b(x−h)) + k
hover to read values

Goes forever. Never stops. The exact same cycle, repeated endlessly with no variation. Bounded: no matter what you put in, the output stays between its maximum and minimum. The only shape that is both infinite and constrained.

Amplitude says how tall. Period says how long one cycle takes. Midline says where it oscillates around. Phase shift says where in the cycle it starts.

domain: all reals range: [k−|a|, k+|a|] period: 2π / b repeats forever
The question: where are you in the cycle? The unit circle is the answer — every angle maps to a point, and sine and cosine are just the coordinates of that point. The wave is the circle, unrolled.

10 The Cross-Sections The Conic Sections  ·  one cone, four shapes
hover to read values

Four completely different-looking curves that all come from slicing the same double cone at different angles. Slice straight across: circle. Tilt the knife: ellipse. Cut parallel to the side: parabola. Go steep: hyperbola.

They all share the same language: (h, k) is the center or vertex, and the sign between squared terms tells you the shape. Plus → ellipse or circle. Minus → hyperbola. Only one variable squared → parabola.

center/vertex (h, k) + sign → ellipse / circle − sign → hyperbola one squared → parabola
The question: what cross-section did you take? Read the equation: count squared terms, check the sign, identify h and k. You've already spent a whole year with the parabola. The cone was there from the beginning.
Conic Sections Explorer Quadratic Forms Explorer
They're all related.

The logarithm and the exponential are exact mirrors of each other — reflect one over y = x and you get the other.

The parabola is a conic section. It was always a slice of a cone, even in September when you first met it as y = ax² + bx + c.

The absolute value function has two branches, just like the radical equation has two candidates — and both require a check for the same reason. Squaring and absolute value both destroy sign information. The inverse has to guess, and sometimes it guesses wrong.

The asymptote of the rational function and the domain restriction of the radical are the same idea: some inputs are forbidden. Division by zero. Negatives under a square root. The same wall, different rooms.

By the end of this year, you can look at any equation and ask:
What kind of shape is this? What's its personality?

And whatever question the problem is asking — find the zero, find the inverse, find the intersection — it's always the same move underneath:

given the output, what was the input?

That's algebra.
